THE CUBE

Voice-controlled physical computing system

A compact hardware and software system combining voice interaction, a 64×64 LED matrix, integrated audio and a custom-designed enclosure around a Raspberry Pi 5.

[002]

Introduction

Cube

Cube is a 128 mm desktop device I am building around a Raspberry Pi 5, a 64×64 RGB matrix, local voice processing and a custom enclosure.

The idea is to use it as a physical voice interface for my computer and home server. It can listen for a wake word, transcribe speech locally, and pass requests to the local AI running on my PC.

From there, the AI can interact with my computer through the terminal, for example executing commands, searching for files or prints, and controlling local tools.

Requests that do not need my PC can be handled through my home server instead. The Cube can trigger existing automations to download movies or music, manage services, or send files back to me.

Before designing the enclosure, I started by testing the individual parts: the HUB75E matrix and animations, wake-word detection, Whisper for speech recognition, and local language models.

The current stage is focused on the internal body: fitting the electronics inside the 128 mm cube, designing mounts, routing cables, managing cooling and testing printed prototypes before moving on to the final enclosure.

Physical Design

Physical Design

Explore the structure, component layout and enclosure of the Cube.

01 / Complete

Internal Body

I designed the internal body specifically around FDM 3D printing. The print orientation was part of the design from the beginning, and I used chamfers, slopes, gradual transitions teardrop-shaped holesto avoid unsupported geometry and keep the main structure support-free.

I also built cable routing into the printed body instead of letting wires sit underneath the boards. The channels keep the wiring in place and prevent cables from touching the underside of components such as the 52Pi board. Mounting points use heat-set inserts so the hardware can be removed and reassembled without wearing out the plastic.

I mounted the Raspberry Pi stack vertically because it worked better for the airflow I wanted. Instead of the air hitting the boards from underneath, it can move upward between and alongside them. Cool air enters from the bottom, passes through the stack, and the fan near the top pulls the warm air out.

Clearances were the part that took the most trial and error. Some dimensions that looked fine in Fusion 360 were too tight once printed, especially around boards, connectors, screws, and cable paths. I went through four prints before everything fit the way I wanted. Each version changed small things like tolerances, mounting positions and cable channels rather than completely redesigning the part.

02 / Complete

Outer Shell

Most of the work went into the proportions, wall thickness, openings, and the way the shell fits around the internal body. I also had to make sure the ports, speaker area, ventilation, and screw access still worked once everything was enclosed.

Just like the internal body, the shell was designed with 3D printing in mind, so tolerances and assembly clearances were part of the CAD from the beginning instead of being fixed afterwards.

03 / Done

Acoustic chamber

I designed and printed a separate chamber to give the speaker its own sealed space inside Cube. To prevent air leaking between the printed plastic parts, I also printed a flexible TPU gasket to seal the joint.

One unexpected challenge was mounting the speaker itself. Its frame had very little surface area at the top and bottom to rest against the chamber, so getting it to sit flat and form a proper seal was more difficult than I expected.
